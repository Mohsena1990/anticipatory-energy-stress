# Findings Report: Anticipatory Fuel Stress Watch (AFSW)

**Forecasting Anticipatory Energy–Carbon Stress and Household Fuel Vulnerability in the UK**

This report synthesizes the results across all five pipeline stages into a single narrative: what the forecasts show, what drives household fuel vulnerability, how it's distributed geographically and demographically, how well the project's own results hold up against an independent external benchmark, and what the forward-looking predictions say. Every figure and table referenced here is analyzed file-by-file in `reports/01_outputs_catalog.md`; this document tells the story those files add up to.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

- UK household fuel vulnerability (fuel spend ≥10% of net income, the UK's standard threshold) declined steadily from **11.57% in 2009 to a low of 5.09% in 2020**, then spiked sharply to **10.83% (2022) and 10.62% (2023)** during the cost-of-living crisis (`policy_vulnerability_by_wave.csv`/`.png`). This is not a modelling artifact — the same shape independently reproduces the Joseph Rowntree Foundation's (JRF) own account of a hardship spike over exactly this window (`external_validation_wave_trend.png`).
- **Northern Ireland is the standout geographic finding of the whole project**: the most fuel-vulnerable region by a wide margin (15.62%, vs. 9.75% for the next-highest, West Midlands — `policy_vulnerability_by_region.csv`), yet JRF's own *least*-income-poor UK nation (17%, lowest of 12 — `jrf_poverty_benchmark_region.csv`). This is fully explained, not left as an anomaly: 71.2% of NI households use oil heating (vs. 1.5–9.8% everywhere else in the UK), and within NI itself, oil-heating households pay 62% more for fuel and are vulnerable at nearly double the rate of non-oil NI households in the same wave and region (`ni_oil_heating_evidence_by_region.csv`, `ni_oil_heating_evidence_within_ni.csv`).
- The strongest driver of vulnerability is, overwhelmingly, **financial/psychological strain** (odds ratio 6.85, `driver_analysis_logistic_regression.csv`), not the forecasted energy-price shock itself (`fes_delta`, non-significant, p=0.182, in the same table, and flat across FES-severity terciles in `policy_vulnerability_by_fes_tier.csv`).
- Two forecast variants (FES_core, using univariate models; FES_macro, using models with exogenous drivers) diverge in an interesting way: **core-mode models won the per-series accuracy race in 5 of 6 series/modes, yet the macro-mode aggregate index validates better against realised stress** on nearly every benchmark (`fes_comparison_metrics.csv`) — an unresolved tension discussed in Section 2.
- A genuinely forward-looking model — trained only on data up to each household's current wave, validated on wave transitions the model never saw during training — achieves **AUC 0.758** at predicting who becomes vulnerable next year (`stage5_validation_metrics.csv`), and scores all 19,140 wave-o households for their own next interview year, finding a small number of households (1.2%) carry very concentrated risk (predicted probability >50%, `stage5_forward_predictions.csv`).
- External validation against JRF's independently-published "UK Poverty 2025" report shows genuine, if imperfect, agreement: strong on housing tenure (Spearman ρ=0.80), strong on region once Northern Ireland's documented fuel-oil driver is set aside (ρ=0.73 vs. 0.33 including it), and weaker on ethnicity (ρ=0.14) — a divergence discussed on its own merits in Section 5, not smoothed over.
- Several methodological caveats surfaced during this analysis are significant enough to affect how confidently specific numbers should be quoted — most importantly, out-of-range SEM fit indices (Section 3) and a non-significant FES-moderation interaction (Section 3) — and are catalogued in full in Section 7.

## 2. Forecasting Performance and the FES Index

The pipeline forecasts UK gas, electricity, and carbon price growth using four models (SARIMA, Prophet, LSTM, TFT) in two modes (core = univariate; macro = plus exogenous macro drivers), then combines the three series into an equal-weighted "Forecasted Energy Stress" (FES) index.

**LSTM wins almost everywhere it's tested against real outcomes.** Across all six series/mode combinations (`model_metrics_comparison.csv`, `model_ranking_polar_*.png`), LSTM produces the lowest realised-2025 RMSE in five of six cells (gas-core 7.60, gas-macro 11.86, electricity-core 4.14, electricity-macro 4.54, carbon-core 15.00). The lone exception is carbon-macro, where **TFT wins clearly** (RMSE 13.79 vs. LSTM's 20.62) — the only case in the entire grid where a macro-mode model beats every core-mode model and every other macro-mode model (`carbon_growth_pct_forecasts_tft_macro.csv`).

**SARIMA and Prophet show clear degenerate behaviour that a backtest alone would not catch.** SARIMA's core-mode forecasts for gas and electricity explode into implausible territory in the second half of 2025 — gas-core reaches 144% against an actual 2.2%, electricity-core reaches 51–55% against an actual 4.6–8.0% — with prediction intervals that widen in step (`forecast_comparison_gas_core.png`, `forecast_comparison_electricity_core.png`). Prophet's carbon-series forecasts are more subtly broken: its prediction interval bounds are *literally frozen* at −99.78%/+137.96% across all 12 months in both modes (`carbon_growth_pct_forecasts_prophet_core.csv`, `_macro.csv`) — a collapsed, non-time-varying uncertainty estimate rather than genuine month-to-month recalibration.

**This produces a genuine hindsight-selection problem.** Because the pipeline's own model-selection step uses `forecast_actual_RMSE` — accuracy computed *after* the 2025 actuals were known — it disagrees with the walk-forward backtest ranking (`rank_score`) in four of six series/mode cells. The starkest case: for electricity-core, the backtest ranks **SARIMA best** (rank_score 1.0), but SARIMA turns out to be the single worst model in the entire 24-cell grid against real 2025 data (RMSE 46.84, an 11× gap versus LSTM's 4.14). A genuinely blind 2025 forecast, chosen only on pre-2025 information, would in several cases have picked a materially worse model than the one this project reports as "the best model" for that series.

**The per-series core/macro selection mechanism (`FES_selected`) has, in this run, never actually chosen macro.** `fes_components_table.csv` and `fes_monthly_2025.csv` confirm `FES_selected` is numerically identical to `FES_core` in every component and every month — even though the model-comparison table shows carbon's true best model is macro-mode TFT. This matters because of a second finding: **`FES_macro`, the full macro-mode aggregate index, validates better against every one of the three realised-stress benchmarks than `FES_core`/`FES_selected` does** (`fes_comparison_metrics.csv`: Pearson r 0.813/0.801/0.778 for macro vs. 0.727/0.742/0.740 for core, across the RollingVol/CrossComp/AbsShock benchmarks respectively). Against the AbsShock benchmark specifically, FES_core/Selected posts a **negative R² (−0.850)** and a **Theil's U of 1.718** — worse than predicting the mean, and worse than a naive persistence forecast. This is a real tension: per-series accuracy favours core, but aggregate-index tracking favours macro, and the current selection logic resolves it in favour of the option that validates worse.

**The realised 2025 FES path itself has a shape neither forecast variant anticipates.** `fes_monthly_2025.png` shows the actual index dipping around March, recovering sharply by April, improving to a September peak, and then **relapsing in October–December** — while both FES_core and FES_macro continue trending toward less-negative (less-stressed) values right through December. This end-of-year miss is the largest single divergence between forecast and reality in the whole Stage 1 output set. On an annual-mean basis, though, the two forecasts bracket the truth reasonably: FES_core undershoots the realised annual mean (−1.061 vs. −1.150) and FES_macro overshoots it (−1.256 vs. −1.150) by similar margins.

**Realised 2025 stress is substantially milder than 2024.** The newly-added prior-year actual baseline (`fes_prior_actual_2024.csv`, `fes_summary_2025.csv`) shows 2024's annual mean FES at −2.582, roughly 2.2× the magnitude of 2025's −1.150 — both forecasts and the realised actual agree directionally that 2025 was a genuine easing year, even though they disagree on 2025's own within-year shape.

## 3. Household Resource Structure: COR-SEM and COR-CVAE

The project operationalizes Hobfoll's Conservation of Resources (COR) theory via a 4-factor confirmatory factor analysis (COR-SEM: Object, Condition, Personal, Energy resources, aggregating into a second-order Baseline Resource Stock) and, separately, a FES-conditioned Conditional VAE (COR-CVAE) producing an aligned 4-dimensional latent space.

**Measurement is solid for three of four factors, weaker for Condition.** `cor_sem_measurement_loadings.csv` shows all 13 items loading significantly (p≈0) in the expected direction, but `bill_security` (std. loading 0.226) falls below the conventional 0.3–0.4 acceptability threshold — visually confirmed against a reference line in `cor_sem_loadings.png` — and Condition is, on the whole, the weakest-measured factor (all three of its items, `tenure_security` 0.372, `jbstat_security` 0.450, `bill_security` 0.226, sit at or near that line). The project's README already documents that adding `bill_security` strengthened the other two Condition items when it was tested, so its retention is a considered trade-off, not an oversight.

**Two fit-index tables report values outside their valid mathematical range**, and this needs to be read carefully rather than taken at face value. `cor_sem_fit_indices.csv` (the measurement model) reports CFI=−0.538 and TLI=−1.033 — both should lie in [0,1], and both are flagged `False` by the pipeline's own validity check. `structural_fit_indices_baseline.csv` (the second-order model) is more severe: SRMR=9,214,088,570.31 (correctly flagged invalid), GFI=−0.735 (out of range, with no corresponding flag), and **CFI=1.594/TLI=1.760 — both exceed 1.0, yet are marked `cfi_ok=True`/`tli_ok=True`**, suggesting the pipeline's validity check tests only a lower bound and lets an upper-bound violation through silently. The project's own documentation attributes this to a known `semopy` 2.3.11 limitation under FIML with unstandardized, very differently-scaled indicators (raw £ values mixed with 0/1 security flags) — a plausible explanation, but one that means **loadings and path coefficients should be trusted here well ahead of these particular fit statistics**, and any external presentation of this SEM should say so explicitly.

**The second-order structural paths show a Heywood-case-like pattern.** `structural_paths_baseline.csv` finds Object loading a plausible, sub-unity 0.712 on the second-order Baseline factor, but Condition (0.993), Personal (0.99997), and Energy (0.99999) all load at essentially 1.0 — three of four first-order resource dimensions appear empirically indistinguishable from the overall Baseline construct, which is not a substantively credible result and is consistent with the same scaling/identification problem implicated in the fit-index anomalies above (`structural_paths_baseline.png` shows this visually as three near-full-height bars against Object's clearly shorter one).

**The central "FES-moderation" hypothesis is not supported by the data.** This is one of the most important corrections this findings report can make relative to how the project is named and described elsewhere: `fes_moderation_path.csv` tests whether Baseline Resource Stock moderates (dampens) the effect of an FES shock on fuel-to-income ratio, via an interaction term. The interaction (`baseline_x_fes`) has a p-value of **0.608** — not remotely significant. Only the two additive main effects hold: higher baseline resources associate with a lower fuel-to-income ratio (coefficient −0.025, p≈0), and higher FES Delta associates with a very small reduction in the ratio (coefficient −0.0006, p≈0). Together they explain just under 11% of variance (R²=0.1085). **The honest finding is additive, not moderating** — low-resource households are not shown here to be differentially more exposed to a price shock than high-resource households; both groups' fuel-to-income ratio responds to Baseline and FES Delta in parallel, not interactively.

**The CVAE's latent space aligns with the SEM factors, but only partially.** `cvae_sem_alignment.csv` shows two of four latent dimensions cleanly dedicated to their intended factor — z2 (condition, r=−0.830, well clear of its next-highest correlate at −0.456) and z3 (personal, r=0.671, clear of its next-highest at 0.439) — while the other two are diffuse: z1 correlates almost as strongly with the *energy* factor (−0.613) as with its own *object* factor (−0.764), and z4 correlates nearly as strongly with *object* (−0.636) and *condition* (−0.594) as with its own *energy* factor (−0.648). The object and energy latent dimensions have not fully disentangled from one another. Three of the four "own-factor" correlations are also negative (only personal is positive) — an arbitrary consequence of VAE latent sign, not an error, but a reminder that raw `cvae_*_z` values are not directly comparable in sign to the SEM `*_score` columns they're named after.

**The counterfactual FES-shift simulation shows a near-null average effect with real underlying heterogeneity.** Swapping each household's own realised FES exposure for the shared forecast shock changes the mean predicted vulnerability probability by only +0.046 percentage points on a ~29% base rate (`cvae_counterfactual_fes_shift.csv`) — but individual shifts range from −5.0 to +5.9 percentage points, meaning the population-level near-null average conceals substantial household-level variation in how sensitive different households are to the shock scenario. This same pattern echoes in Stage 4's Map 3 (Section 4): every region shifts in the same direction under the shared shock, but by very different amounts.

## 4. Who Is Vulnerable, and Why

Vulnerability is identified two ways — a fuzzy c-means "Resource Depleted" membership score, and a one-class SVM anomaly score trained on a resilient reference group — both validated against the objective fuel-to-income ratio threshold (`stage3_validation_against_objective_ratio.csv`). **The two methods are not equally useful**: fuzzy c-means achieves AUC 0.750 against the true label, a genuinely useful discriminator, while the one-class SVM manages only AUC 0.567 (barely above chance) and a near-zero Spearman correlation (0.015) — it appears to be detecting a different, more general kind of "unusualness" in the household's profile than fuel poverty specifically.

**Financial and psychological strain is, by a wide margin, the dominant driver of vulnerability.** The national driver analysis (`driver_analysis_logistic_regression.csv`, n=103,646) finds `financial_strain_score` at odds ratio 6.85 — several times larger than any other predictor. `jbstat_security` (employment security) is the strongest protective factor (OR 0.13), followed by `qfhigh_band` (education level, OR 0.58). **The forecasted energy-price shock itself, `fes_delta`, is not a significant predictor of household-level vulnerability** (OR 1.02, p=0.182) — a finding that recurs independently in `policy_vulnerability_by_fes_tier.csv`, where vulnerability is essentially flat (7.8%/8.0%/7.9%) across low/moderate/high national FES-stress terciles. Household-level vulnerability, in this data, is overwhelmingly a function of the household's own financial and employment circumstances, not of the macro energy-price cycle.

Two logistic-regression signs are worth flagging as genuinely counter-intuitive rather than glossing over: `health_good` (self-rated health "good") and `hsrooms`/`hsbeds` (more rooms/bedrooms) all *raise* the odds of vulnerability. The most plausible reading — larger, older homes cost more to heat, and better self-rated health may correlate with older homeowners in exactly this kind of housing stock — is offered in the driver-analysis figures (`policy_driver_odds_ratios.png`) but not tested directly here, and would be a natural next analysis.

**Driver effect sizes vary substantially by region** (`driver_analysis_by_region.csv`, `driver_analysis_by_region.png`). Financial strain's odds ratio ranges from 14.67 in Northern Ireland and 14.57 in South East down to just 2.86 in London — a roughly 5× spread. Smaller-sample regions (Northern Ireland, n=1,879; North East, n=4,577) carry correspondingly wider uncertainty around their region-specific coefficients, a caveat worth remembering before treating any single region's driver profile as precisely estimated.

## 5. Geography, Ethnicity, Disability, and Tenure

**Regional pattern.** Northern Ireland (15.62%) is the standout, more than 5.8 percentage points above the next-highest region (West Midlands, 9.75%), and roughly 2.6× the lowest (South East, 5.96%) — `policy_vulnerability_by_region.csv`, `policy_vulnerability_by_region_map.png`. The Midlands/North/Wales form a contiguous mid-vulnerability band, while the southern English regions (South East, South West, East of England) sit persistently lowest across nearly every regional table in this project — the by-wave breakdown, the temporal-change map (`policy_temporal_change_map.png`, where South West is the *only* region to worsen, +0.23pp, while Northern Ireland improves by far the most, −10.21pp), and both policy-geography maps in Stage 4.

**Ethnicity, disability, and tenure — added this session specifically to make the project's outputs comparable to external poverty statistics — reveal a genuinely informative, non-uniform picture.** By ethnicity (`policy_vulnerability_by_ethnicity.csv`), Black Caribbean households rank highest (14.06%) and Any other Asian background lowest (6.66%); White households, who make up the large majority of the sample (265,950 of ~339k rows), sit toward the lower end (7.76%). By disability (`policy_vulnerability_by_disability.csv`), households containing a disabled adult are more vulnerable (12.86%) than those without (10.33%) — directionally as expected. By tenure (`policy_vulnerability_by_tenure.csv`), the ranking is Social renting (11.28%) > **Owned outright (9.87%)** > Private renting (8.50%) > Buying with mortgage (4.15%) — with Owned outright's position above Private renting being the one genuinely surprising result: a fuel-to-income measure, unlike an income-poverty measure, picks up asset-rich-but-heating-cost-exposed households (often older, outright owners in older housing stock) that a pure income lens does not flag as poor.

These three breakdowns were deliberately built as descriptive strata, not folded into the SEM/CVAE as new latent constructs and not added as covariates to the existing driver-analysis regression — ethnicity and disability are demographic/health covariates, not reflective indicators of an underlying continuous resource construct, and several ethnicity categories are small-sample nationally (e.g. Chinese n=1,408, Any other Black background n=426), which would have destabilized an already-validated regression for uncertain benefit.

## 6. External Validation Against JRF's UK Poverty 2025

This project's fuel-to-income vulnerability measure and JRF's relative income-poverty rate (after housing costs) are, by design, different constructs — they should broadly agree in direction while disagreeing in specifics, and both kinds of result are informative. The validation (`src/ukhls_external_validation.py`) checks four dimensions plus one time-series comparison.

**Tenure shows the strongest agreement** (Spearman ρ=0.80, Pearson r=0.68, n=4 — `external_validation_tenure_comparison.csv`, `external_validation_tenure_scatter.png`), with Owned outright as the one notable rank-swap (2nd on this project's measure vs. 3rd on JRF's), consistent with the fuel-vs-income distinction discussed in Section 5.

**Region shows strong agreement once Northern Ireland is set aside** (ρ=0.33/r=−0.10 for all 12 regions vs. ρ=0.73/r=0.68 excluding NI — `external_validation_region_comparison.csv`, `external_validation_region_scatter.png`). This is the project's headline validation finding, and it was investigated rather than assumed: Northern Ireland ranks #1 (most vulnerable) on this project's measure but #12 (least income-poor) of 12 UK nations/regions on JRF's own published rate. Rather than treat this as noise, the project verified the mechanism directly against its own data — 71.2% of NI households use oil heating, a rate 7.3× higher than the next-highest GB region (Wales, 9.8%), and, in a controlled within-NI comparison isolating this one factor, oil-heating NI households pay 62% more annually for fuel (£2,017 vs. £1,243) and are vulnerable at nearly double the rate (20.9% vs. 12.3%) of non-oil NI households in the same wave and region (`ni_oil_heating_evidence_by_region.csv`, `ni_oil_heating_evidence_within_ni.csv`). Heating oil is bought in volatile-priced lump sums and sits outside Ofgem's electricity/gas price cap — a genuine, well-isolated fuel-specific cost exposure with no reason to appear in an income-based poverty measure. Northern Ireland is therefore excluded only from the region correlation coefficient (a like-for-like check of construct agreement), never from the underlying regional analysis itself, where its result is arguably the single most policy-relevant finding in the whole regional breakdown.

**Ethnicity shows the weakest agreement** (Spearman ρ=0.14, Pearson r=0.27, n=6 — `external_validation_ethnicity_comparison.csv`, `external_validation_ethnicity_scatter.png`), and the divergence runs in both directions: Bangladeshi households are JRF's #1 most income-poor ethnic group (56%) but rank only #4 of 6 on this project's fuel measure (9.50%), while Black Caribbean households are the mirror case — #1 on this project's measure (14.06%) but #5 of 6 on JRF's income measure (30%). This is reported as a genuine, unresolved divergence between two different constructs of hardship, worth further investigation (e.g. housing-stock age/type, household composition, or heating-fuel-mix differences by ethnicity), not dismissed or smoothed over.

**Disability shows directionally consistent but statistically inconclusive agreement** (both measures rank "contains a disabled adult"/"disabled adults" above the alternative, but with only 2 categories the correlation coefficient is mathematically undefined — `external_validation_disability_comparison.csv` — and both figures display "ρ=nan"/"r=nan" in their titles, a technically correct but potentially confusing result that would benefit from an explanatory caption if shown to a non-technical audience).

**The wave-level time trend independently reproduces JRF's own account of the cost-of-living crisis** (`external_validation_wave_trend.png`). JRF's own slow-moving annual relative-poverty measure stayed "broadly flat" 2021/22→2022/23 by its own account, while its faster cost-of-living hardship tracker showed the share of low-income households going without essentials peaking around October 2022 before easing only partially by October 2024. This project's fuel-to-income vulnerability rate — an independently constructed measure — shows the same shape: 6.31% (2021) → 10.83% (2022) → 10.62% (2023), a sharp spike followed by only partial easing, matching JRF's *faster*, more responsive indicator rather than its slower one. This is a meaningful validation that the measure is sensitive to the right real-world event at the right time.

## 7. Forward-Looking Prediction

Stage 5 answers a genuinely different, prospective question from every earlier stage: not who is vulnerable now, but who is about to become vulnerable, using only information available before the outcome is known.

**The model discriminates well on data it never trained on.** Walk-forward validated on the two most recent held-out wave transitions (m→n, n→o), the logistic model achieves **AUC 0.758** against the true next-wave vulnerability outcome (`stage5_validation_metrics.csv`, `stage5_validation_roc.png`) — a solid result for a cross-sectional predictor of a genuinely future, unobserved-at-training-time outcome. Its correlation with the continuous fuel-to-income ratio is weaker (Pearson r=0.265), suggesting the model is considerably better at classifying who crosses the vulnerability threshold than at explaining the magnitude of financial strain.

**The forecast signal (`fes_magnitude`) is, by a wide margin, the single strongest predictor in the final model** (odds ratio 52.0, `stage5_driver_coefficients.csv`) — far larger than `financial_strain_score` (OR 4.86), the dominant driver in the contemporaneous Stage 3 analysis. This is worth reading carefully: `fes_magnitude_used` in the actual household-level predictions (`stage5_forward_predictions.csv`) is a single constant (−1.0608) applied identically to every one of the 19,140 households — so while the *coefficient* shows the forecast signal matters enormously in the model's structure, it cannot by itself explain any of the *variation* between households or regions in the resulting predictions; that differentiation comes entirely from the other seven covariates (financial strain, the four COR-SEM factor scores, age, and central heating).

**Applying the model to the most recent wave produces a right-skewed risk distribution with a small, concentrated high-risk group.** Mean predicted probability is 6.89%, but the median is only 4.01% — most households are low-risk, while 228 households (1.2%) are predicted above 50% probability and 3,555 (18.6%) above 10% (`stage5_forward_predictions.csv`). This concentrated tail is an obvious candidate for targeted intervention, distinct from a purely geographic targeting approach.

**Regionally and seasonally, the forward predictions largely reproduce known patterns rather than reshuffling them.** Northern Ireland is again highest (7.74%) and South West lowest (6.18%) in the forward-predicted map (`stage5_forward_prediction_map.csv`/`.png`, redesigned this session to display percentages rather than a 2-decimal fraction that previously made most regions round to an indistinguishable "0.07") — the same ordering as the historical prevalence data, just compressed in range (1.56 percentage points forward vs. 9.66 points historically, reflecting model shrinkage toward the shared constant shock term). By target month, April carries the highest predicted risk (7.36%) and September the lowest (6.44%) — a 0.92-percentage-point spread with winter/early-spring months generally running higher than mid/late-summer months (`stage5_forward_prediction_by_month.csv`/`.png`).

## 8. Cross-Cutting Methodological Caveats

These are collected here for visibility; each is detailed with exact numbers in the relevant section above and in the file-by-file catalog (`reports/01_outputs_catalog.md`).

1. Model selection in Stage 1 is effectively a hindsight choice (uses post-hoc realised accuracy), which disagrees with genuine walk-forward backtest rankings in 4 of 6 series/mode cells.
2. `FES_selected` is, in this run, identical to `FES_core` in every respect — the intended per-series core/macro selection has not yet actually selected macro for any series.
3. The aggregate FES_macro index validates better against realised stress than FES_core/Selected, an unresolved tension with the per-series accuracy comparison.
4. Two SEM fit-index tables contain values outside their mathematically valid range (CFI/TLI outside [0,1] in one table; an SRMR of ~9.2 billion in another), and the pipeline's own validity flags do not catch an upper-bound violation.
5. The second-order SEM structural model shows a Heywood-case-like pattern (three of four first-order factors loading ~0.99–1.00 on the second-order Baseline factor).
6. The FES-moderation interaction term is not statistically significant (p=0.608) — only additive main effects are supported by this analysis.
7. The CVAE's latent space aligns cleanly with two of the four intended SEM factors; the other two (object, energy) remain partially entangled with each other.
8. Two output tables are byte-identical duplicates (`policy_map_baseline_resource_by_region.csv` and `policy_map_financial_strain_by_region.csv`).
9. A likely data-sparsity artifact appears in the region×year heatmap (Northern Ireland's 2024 cell reads exactly 0.0%).
10. Two chart titles state a "2024" endpoint where the underlying wave data extends only to 2023.
11. Ethnicity is attributed via the household reference person only (not every household member), and disability is observed only for responding adults — both are documented simplifications, not full-household measures.

None of these caveats overturn the project's central, cross-validated findings (the cost-of-living-crisis spike, the strength of financial strain as a driver, Northern Ireland's fuel-oil-driven outlier status, or the Stage 5 model's genuine forward discriminative power) — but they should inform exactly how confidently specific secondary numbers (particular SEM fit statistics, the FES-moderation framing, the FES_macro-vs-core tension) are quoted in any external-facing use of this work.

## 9. What This Body of Work Establishes

Taken together, the five stages support a coherent account: household fuel vulnerability in the UK is driven overwhelmingly by household-level financial circumstances rather than by the macro energy-price cycle itself; it has a clear geographic and demographic structure that partly but not fully overlaps with income-poverty as conventionally measured, with Northern Ireland's oil-heating exposure standing out as the clearest case where a fuel-specific lens sees something an income-based lens does not; and a model trained only on information available before the fact can meaningfully anticipate, a year ahead, which households are moving toward vulnerability — with real, if compressed, geographic and seasonal structure, and a small concentrated group carrying disproportionate predicted risk. The external validation against JRF's independently-produced statistics is not perfect, but where it disagrees, the disagreement is itself informative rather than merely noisy, and where it agrees, it agrees on both level (tenure, region-excluding-NI) and timing (the wave-level cost-of-living-crisis trend) — a meaningfully stronger form of validation than internal consistency checks alone could provide.
