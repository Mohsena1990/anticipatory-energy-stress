# Findings Report: Anticipatory Fuel Stress Watch (AFSW)

**Forecasting Anticipatory Energy–Carbon Stress and Household Fuel Vulnerability in the UK**

This report synthesizes the results across all five pipeline stages into a single narrative: what the forecasts show and where they fail, what the household resource model reveals (and where its statistics should be read with caution), what drives household fuel vulnerability, how it's distributed geographically and demographically, how well the project's own results hold up against an independent external benchmark, and what the forward-looking predictions say. Every figure and table referenced here is analyzed file-by-file, with exact numbers, in `reports/01_outputs_catalog.md`; this document tells the story those files add up to, and goes further where the underlying data supports additional analysis.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

- UK household fuel vulnerability (fuel spend ≥10% of net income, the UK's standard threshold) declined steadily from **11.57% in 2009 to a low of 5.09% in 2020**, then spiked sharply to **10.83% (2022) and 10.62% (2023)** during the cost-of-living crisis (`policy_vulnerability_by_wave.csv`/`.png`). This is not a modelling artifact — the same shape independently reproduces the Joseph Rowntree Foundation's (JRF) own account of a hardship spike over exactly this window (`external_validation_wave_trend.png`).
- **Northern Ireland is the standout geographic finding of the whole project**, and it recurs, in a different guise, in most Stage 3/4/5 outputs: highest historical vulnerability (`policy_vulnerability_by_region.csv`, 15.62%), one of three regions (with Wales and West Midlands) in the "High resource / High vulnerability" tier — nominally the single *highest*-baseline-resource region of all 12, despite also carrying the highest vulnerability rate, a pairing that should be read alongside the sign-instability caveat in Section 3.2 (`map1_resource_stress_hotspot.csv`) — the most polarized/least-borderline fuzzy-membership distribution (`policy_map2_fuzzy_membership.csv`), the largest longitudinal improvement (`policy_temporal_change_by_region.csv`, −10.21pp), and still the highest forward-predicted risk (`stage5_forward_prediction_map.csv`) — yet it is JRF's own *least*-income-poor UK nation (17%, lowest of 12). (Its simulated forecast-shock vector-shift, Map 3, is the one metric where NI is currently unremarkable/mid-ranked rather than exceptional — see Section 5.3.) This is fully explained, not left as an anomaly: 71.2% of NI households use oil heating (vs. 0.1–9.8% everywhere else in the UK), and within NI itself, oil-heating households pay 62% more for fuel and are vulnerable at nearly double the rate of non-oil NI households in the same wave and region (`ni_oil_heating_evidence_by_region.csv`, `ni_oil_heating_evidence_within_ni.csv`).
- The strongest driver of vulnerability is, overwhelmingly, **financial/psychological strain** (odds ratio 6.79, `driver_analysis_logistic_regression.csv`) — but the forecasted energy-price shock itself (`fes_delta`) is **also a significant, independent driver** (OR 0.94, p<0.0001), just a far smaller effect. This is a correction relative to an earlier snapshot of this analysis, which reported `fes_delta` as non-significant (p=0.182); re-running the pipeline on the current, corrected panel shows it significant and consistently so across every stage that tests it (Stage 2b's OLS main effect, this driver model, and Stage 5's forward-prediction model) — see Section 3.3 and Section 8 for the full, corrected picture.
- Model selection now defaults to genuine walk-forward validation (`selection_basis='validation'`), not post-hoc hindsight accuracy — a methodological fix this iteration. Across the 16-year rolling walk-forward (the reported path), **LSTM wins 52% of the 96 series/mode/year cells**, dominant in carbon (78%) and in core mode generally (63% vs. macro's 42%); no model wins every year for any series. Of the four *composite FES index* variants now compared, **`Equal_Macro` wins the 16-year mean-RMSE comparison** (2.749, `fes_variant_selection.csv`) narrowly ahead of the newly-added `Equal_Weighted` (2.790), with the un-selected `Equal_Core` clearly weakest (3.236) — while on the single most recent (2025) target year specifically, it is instead the per-series-selecting `Equal_Selected`/`Equal_Weighted` variants that decisively beat `Equal_Core`/`Equal_Macro` (Section 2.2). Underlying both comparisons is a deeper problem: two of the four forecasting models (SARIMA, Prophet) show clear degenerate behaviour — SARIMA's core-mode forecasts explode to implausible magnitudes, and Prophet's carbon-series uncertainty bands are frozen constants rather than genuinely time-varying — that a simple backtest ranking does not catch.
- A genuinely forward-looking model — trained only on data up to each household's current wave, validated on wave transitions the model never saw during training — achieves **AUC≈0.76** (0.7615 in the current run; this figure has fluctuated in the 0.756–0.762 range across successive refits of the same specification, not a sign of instability in the underlying finding) at predicting who becomes vulnerable next year (`stage5_validation_metrics.csv`), and scores all 19,140 wave-o households for their own next interview year, finding a small number of households (1.1%) carry very concentrated risk (predicted probability >50%, `stage5_forward_predictions.csv`).
- External validation against JRF's independently-published "UK Poverty 2025" report shows genuine, if imperfect, agreement: strong on housing tenure (Spearman ρ=0.80), strong on region once Northern Ireland's documented fuel-oil driver is set aside (ρ=0.73 vs. 0.33 including it), and weaker on ethnicity (ρ=0.14) — a divergence discussed on its own merits in Section 6, not smoothed over.
- Several methodological caveats surfaced during this analysis are significant enough to affect how confidently specific numbers should be quoted — most importantly, out-of-range SEM fit indices and a **sign-unstable** FES-moderation interaction (Section 3), corrected from an earlier draft's stronger "not supported" claim — and are catalogued in full in Section 8, along with a changelog of two data-pipeline fixes made this iteration that changed several downstream numbers (notably `fes_delta`'s significance in the Stage 3 driver model, and Stage 5's `fes_magnitude` coefficient).

## 2. Forecasting Performance and the FES Index

The pipeline forecasts UK gas, electricity, and carbon price growth using four models (SARIMA, Prophet, LSTM, TFT) in two modes (core = univariate; macro = plus exogenous macro drivers), then combines the three series into an equal-weighted "Forecasted Energy Stress" (FES) index.

### 2.1 Model performance and failure modes

**LSTM dominates the 16-year reported record, but not uniformly.** The rolling walk-forward path (`model_selection_by_year.csv`, `forecast_performance_by_year.csv` — the methodologically preferred, reported results per `reports/06_methodology.md` Section 2.3) retrains and re-selects fresh every year, 2010→2025: LSTM wins **50 of 96** series/mode/year cells (52%), SARIMA 18 (19%), TFT 17 (18%), Prophet 11 (11%). LSTM's dominance is strongest in carbon (25/32 cells, 78%) and core mode generally (30/48, 63%, vs. 20/48 in macro mode) — macro mode is specifically where SARIMA is more competitive (13 of its 18 wins). No model wins every year even in its strongest cell (carbon-core has 3 non-LSTM winners across 16 years), confirming the project's own rationale for re-selecting fresh each year rather than fixing one "best" model. On the single most recent target year (2025, the diagnostic path used for the more granular per-file analysis below), the winners are more scattered: LSTM wins gas-core/gas-macro/electricity-macro, TFT wins electricity-core (6.01, narrowly ahead of LSTM's 6.80) and carbon-macro (19.11, narrowly ahead of Prophet's 19.36), Prophet wins carbon-core (9.93) — a healthy reminder that a single year's winners needn't match the 16-year pattern.

**SARIMA and Prophet show two distinct and instructive failure modes, visible in both the rolling and single-year records.** SARIMA's failure is a *point-forecast* failure: in the 2025 diagnostic run, its core-mode forecasts for gas and electricity explode into implausible territory in the second half of the year — gas-core reaches 144% against an actual 2.2%, electricity-core reaches 72% against an actual 2.7–8.0% — with prediction intervals that widen in lockstep, so wide that they nominally "cover" the actual only 25% of the time despite their size (`forecast_comparison_gas_core.png`, `forecast_comparison_electricity_core.png`). SARIMA's carbon forecasts fail differently: they stay flat and negative (~−11%) all year, missing the entire Q3–Q4 recovery (2 of 12 correct signs), with a prediction interval so wide (167–169 points) it achieves 100% nominal coverage — technically "correct" but analytically useless. Prophet's failure is a *degenerate-uncertainty* failure, most visible on carbon: its prediction-interval bounds are **literally frozen** at −99.78%/+137.96% across all 12 months in both core and macro mode (`carbon_growth_pct_forecasts_prophet_core.csv`, `_macro.csv`) — the identical frozen-band artifact persists in this run even though Prophet's *point* forecast happens to win carbon-core this year. Both failure modes matter for different reasons: SARIMA's would mislead anyone reading only the point forecast; Prophet's would mislead anyone reading only the interval width as a genuine (rather than default/fallback) measure of uncertainty.

**A genuine hindsight-selection problem was identified and fixed this iteration.** An earlier version of this pipeline selected models using `forecast_actual_RMSE` — accuracy computed *after* the target year's actuals were known — which disagreed with the honest walk-forward backtest ranking (`rank_score`) in most series/mode cells. `forecast_pipeline`'s default `selection_basis` is now `'validation'` (pre-target-year information only), and `run_rolling` (the reported path) never supports anything else. The disagreement itself is still visible as a property of the underlying models — e.g. carbon-core's backtest winner (TFT, validation RMSE 14.97) is realised-accuracy's *worst* performer (45.78) in the 2025 diagnostic run, while Prophet (backtest's worst, 44.64) turns out realised-best (9.93) — but it is no longer what the pipeline's own reported "best model" choice is based on. `outputs/tables/model_metrics_comparison.csv` on disk still shows the old, hindsight `forecast_actual` basis because that file was deliberately regenerated with it passed explicitly, specifically to keep this disagreement inspectable (`reports/01_outputs_catalog.md`'s Stage 1 section, "Critical finding").

### 2.2 The core/macro/selected/weighted comparison inside the FES index

**A new, data-driven `FES_weighted` variant has been added alongside the three existing equal-weighted ones** (Core, Macro, and per-series-best-of-core/macro Selected), weighting each series' contribution by the inverse of its own walk-forward validation RMSE. Two separate comparisons now exist, and — genuinely — they disagree on which single variant is "best," a finding reported rather than smoothed over:

- **On the 16-year rolling mean** (`fes_variant_selection.csv`, N=48 comparisons per variant): `Equal_Macro` wins (mean RMSE 2.749), narrowly ahead of `Equal_Weighted` (2.790) and more clearly ahead of `Equal_Selected` (2.940) and `Equal_Core` (3.236, the weakest).
- **On the single most recent (2025) target year** (`fes_comparison_metrics.csv`, N=12): `Equal_Selected` and `Equal_Weighted` decisively beat `Equal_Core`/`Equal_Macro` on every metric, every benchmark — Pearson r up to 0.839 (Selected) vs. as low as 0.102 (Macro/CrossComp), MAE cut by roughly 3–4× (e.g. AbsShock MAE 0.654/0.482 for Selected/Weighted vs. 2.144/1.444 for Core/Macro).

This reversal from an earlier run — where a bug meant `Equal_Selected` was silently identical to `Equal_Core` (the per-series mode-selection mechanism had, in practice, never picked macro for any series, even when a series' true best model was macro-mode) — is itself the more important finding: fixing that bug alone moved `Equal_Selected` from "no better than Core" to "the single best-correlated variant" on the most recent year. The remaining Macro-wins-on-average / Selected-wins-this-year tension is plausibly just a small-sample effect (N=12 vs. N=16×12 months of evidence) rather than a genuine contradiction, but is reported as an open question rather than resolved.

### 2.3 Where the realised 2025 path diverges from the forecasts

**`FES_core` and `FES_macro` now get 2025's basic *direction* wrong, not just its shape.** `fes_monthly_2025.png`/`fes_monthly_2025.csv` show `fes_core` positive in every single month of 2025 (+0.64 to +1.17 — comfortably *below*-average stress, in the pipeline's own scoring) while `fes_actual` is negative in every single month (−0.79 to −1.79 — genuinely *above*-average stress) — the two lines never cross. `fes_macro` oscillates around zero rather than trending either way. Only `fes_selected` and `fes_weighted` land on the correct (negative) side of zero every month, though both still understate the actual's magnitude, especially early in the year. Within that negative actual series, the same non-monotonic shape flagged in an earlier run persists: a dip around March, a jump by April, a peak (least-negative) around July–September, and **a relapse in October–December** that none of the four forecast variants anticipates.

**Realised 2025 stress is substantially milder than 2024, though the exact 2024 baseline was itself corrected this iteration.** `_compute_actual_fes_for_window`'s reference window was fixed to genuinely exclude the prior year from its own z-scoring baseline (an earlier version was partly in-sample) — 2024's annual mean FES is now −2.695 (previously reported as −2.582), roughly 2.3× the magnitude of 2025's −1.150. The direction of the finding (2025 was a genuine easing year relative to 2024) is unchanged; only the precise magnitude of 2024's own severity moved.

## 3. Household Resource Structure: COR-SEM and COR-CVAE

The project operationalizes Hobfoll's Conservation of Resources (COR) theory via a 4-factor confirmatory factor analysis (COR-SEM: Object, Condition, Personal, Energy resources, aggregating into a second-order Baseline Resource Stock) and, separately, a FES-conditioned Conditional VAE (COR-CVAE) producing an aligned 4-dimensional latent space.

### 3.1 Measurement quality

**Measurement is solid for three of four factors, weaker for Condition.** `cor_sem_measurement_loadings.csv` shows all 13 items loading significantly (p≈0) in the expected direction, but `bill_security` (std. loading 0.226) falls below the conventional 0.3–0.4 acceptability threshold — visually confirmed against a reference line in `cor_sem_loadings.png` — and Condition is, on the whole, the weakest-measured factor (all three of its items, `tenure_security` 0.372, `jbstat_security` 0.450, `bill_security` 0.226, sit at or near that line). The project's own documentation already records that adding `bill_security` strengthened the other two Condition items when it was tested (tenure_security's loading rose from 0.254 to 0.372, jbstat_security's from 0.314 to 0.449), so its retention despite its own weak loading is a considered trade-off, not an oversight — a useful example of how a measurement decision can be net-positive for a factor even while one item within it under-performs individually.

### 3.2 Fit-index reliability, and a second, more consequential instability

**Two fit-index tables report values outside their valid mathematical range**, and this needs to be read carefully rather than taken at face value. `cor_sem_fit_indices.csv` (the measurement model) reports CFI/TLI outside [0,1] and is flagged `False` by the pipeline's own validity check; `structural_fit_indices_baseline.csv` (the second-order model) shows an SRMR many orders of magnitude too large, correctly flagged invalid. The project's own documentation attributes this to a known `semopy` 2.3.11 limitation under FIML with unstandardized, very differently-scaled indicators (raw £ values mixed with 0/1 security flags) — a plausible explanation, but one that means **loadings, not these fit statistics, should be trusted as the primary evidence for measurement quality**.

**A second, more consequential instability was found this iteration by deliberately re-running Stage 2b twice on materially the same data** — once to verify an unrelated bug fix, once as part of a full pipeline rerun minutes later. Both refits reported CFI/TLI in the invalid range as expected, but **also produced opposite signs for the interaction term central to Section 3.3 below** (+0.00049 in one refit, −0.00073 in the other, both p<0.0001). The second-order structural paths explain the mechanism: `structural_paths_baseline.csv` shows CONDITION/PERSONAL/ENERGY's loadings on the second-order BASELINE factor converging to roughly −500 to −1200 (standardized ≈ −1.0) against OBJECT's loading, which is fixed at +1.0 only by identification convention — not estimated from data. Flipping every one of the three free loadings' sign simultaneously fits the data identically well, so the direction "more BASELINE resources" points is arbitrary under the current specification, and this arbitrariness propagates directly into whichever regression uses `baseline_score`. Practically, this means the second-order "Baseline Resource Stock" score, wherever it is used downstream (Stage 3's driver analysis, Stage 4's resource-stress hotspot map), should be read as a composite whose *overall direction* is not yet reliably pinned down, not merely as a numerically imbalanced one — a stronger caveat than earlier drafts of this report carried, and one that specifically requires an explicit sign constraint on the second-order model (not yet implemented) before it is resolved.

### 3.3 The moderation hypothesis: significant, but not yet trustworthy

**Whether baseline resources specifically buffer an FES shock is not currently established, in either direction — this supersedes an earlier, more confident "not supported" conclusion in a prior draft of this report.** `fes_moderation_path.csv` tests whether Baseline Resource Stock moderates (dampens) the effect of an FES shock on fuel-to-income ratio, via an interaction term (`baseline_x_fes`). An earlier snapshot of this analysis found the interaction non-significant (p=0.61). The current, corrected pipeline finds it **significant in every recent refit** (p<0.0001) — but, per Section 3.2 above, **its sign is not stable across those same refits** (+0.00049 then −0.00073). A result that is significant but sign-flipping across identical reruns is not evidence of a real, reproducible moderation effect in either direction; it is evidence the underlying second-order factor is not yet identified. What *is* stable across every refit: `fes_delta`'s own main effect (coefficient consistently −0.0006 to −0.0009, p<0.0001, always negative) and `baseline_score`'s main effect (also always significant, though its own sign inherits the same instability as the interaction — see Section 3.2). **The honest current finding is: FES matters, baseline resources matter, but whether resources specifically cushion households against an FES shock (as opposed to each mattering independently) is an open question this model cannot yet answer** — not the confident null the project's evidence appeared to support in an earlier iteration. This is corroborated, for the *main effect* only, in Section 4 below: `fes_delta` is now also significant in the Stage 3 driver-analysis logistic regression (OR 0.94, p<0.0001), reversing an earlier finding of non-significance there too (see Section 8's changelog).

### 3.4 CVAE alignment and the counterfactual simulation

**The CVAE's latent space aligns with the SEM factors for three of four dimensions — and has gotten worse, not better, for the fourth.** `cvae_sem_alignment.csv` (current run) shows z1 (object, r=−0.79, clear of its next-highest at −0.62), z2 (condition, r=0.80, clear of its next-highest at 0.56), and z3 (personal, r=0.75, clear of its next-highest at 0.39) all cleanly dedicated to their intended factor — each an improvement in separation over an earlier run of this same architecture. **z4 has moved from "partially entangled with object" to actively mis-aligned**: its correlation with `object_score` (0.75) now *exceeds* its correlation with its own intended factor, `energy_score` (0.69). This is a new, worse instance of the same object/energy entanglement flagged in an earlier draft of this report, not a resolved issue — and, combined with the `object_score` sign anomaly noted in Section 4 below, suggests the object/asset dimension is genuinely harder for both estimation methods (SEM and CVAE alike) to cleanly separate from the rest of the resource construct. Raw sign is an arbitrary consequence of VAE latent orientation, not an error — only the ranking of |r| within each latent dimension's row is meaningful, and `cvae_*_z` values are not directly comparable in sign to the SEM `*_score` columns they're named after without an explicit sign-flip step.

**The counterfactual FES-shift simulation shows a near-null average effect with real underlying heterogeneity.** Swapping each household's own realised FES exposure for the shared forecast shock changes the mean predicted vulnerability probability by essentially nothing (−0.001 percentage points, effectively zero) on a ~28% base rate (`cvae_counterfactual_fes_shift.csv`) — but individual shifts range from −4.6 to +3.8 percentage points, meaning the population-level near-null average conceals substantial household-level variation in how sensitive different households are to the shock scenario. A loosely similar pattern shows up in Stage 4's Map 3 (Section 5.3 below): regions do *not* all shift in the same direction under the shared shock (five rise slightly, seven fall), the shifts are uniformly tiny (all under 0.015 percentage points), and the region with the *smallest* shift (South West) and the *largest* (London) are not the ones current vulnerability levels alone would predict — nor, per Section 5.3, has this specific regional ranking proven stable across successive reruns of the same simulation.

## 4. Who Is Vulnerable, and Why

Vulnerability is identified two ways — a fuzzy c-means "Resource Depleted" membership score, and a one-class SVM anomaly score trained on a resilient reference group — both validated against the objective fuel-to-income ratio threshold (`stage3_validation_against_objective_ratio.csv`). **The two methods are not equally useful**: fuzzy c-means achieves AUC 0.744 against the true label, a genuinely useful discriminator, while the one-class SVM manages only AUC 0.62 (modestly above chance) and a weak but real Spearman correlation (0.13) — it appears to be detecting a different, more general kind of "unusualness" in the household's profile than fuel poverty specifically, though less exclusively so than in an earlier run. Among the 253,913 households with valid scores, hard cluster assignment (via arg-max of the three fuzzy memberships) splits 34.4% "resilient," 38.0% "vulnerable to loss," and 27.6% "resource depleted" (`stage3_vulnerability_scores.csv`) — a reminder that most of the panel sits in the "resilient" or "at risk but not yet depleted" categories at any given wave, consistent with the pooled national vulnerability rate sitting around 7–12% across most years.

**Financial and psychological strain is, by a wide margin, the dominant driver of vulnerability — but the forecasted shock is now a confirmed, if much smaller, independent driver too.** The national driver analysis (`driver_analysis_logistic_regression.csv`, n=103,621, now 17 covariates — extended this iteration with `large_family`, `lone_parent`, `workless_household`, and the equivalisation factor `ieqmoecd_dv`) finds `financial_strain_score` at odds ratio 6.79 — still several times larger than any other predictor. `jbstat_security` (employment security) is the strongest protective factor (OR 0.16), followed by `ieqmoecd_dv` (OR 0.28) and `qfhigh_band` (education level, OR 0.50). **`fes_delta` is now significant** (OR 0.94, p<0.0001) — correcting an earlier draft of this report, which found it non-significant (OR 1.02, p=0.182) on a version of the panel affected by a data-pipeline bug since fixed (see Section 8). The effect is real but small relative to financial strain — about a 6% odds reduction per unit of `fes_delta`, versus financial strain's near-7-fold effect — so the substantive policy conclusion is unchanged even though the earlier "not significant at all" framing was not: household-level vulnerability is still overwhelmingly a function of the household's own financial and employment circumstances, with the macro energy-price signal contributing a real but comparatively minor independent effect on top.

Three logistic-regression signs are worth flagging as genuinely counter-intuitive rather than glossing over: `health_good` (self-rated health "good") and `hsrooms`/`hsbeds` (more rooms/bedrooms) all *raise* the odds of vulnerability, and this now recurs independently in Stage 5's forward-prediction model (`object_score`, OR 1.43 — Section 7). The most plausible reading — larger, older homes cost more to heat, and better self-rated health may correlate with older homeowners in exactly this kind of housing stock — is offered in the driver-analysis figures (`policy_driver_odds_ratios.png`) but not tested directly here, and would be a natural next analysis (e.g. interacting `hsrooms`/`hsbeds` with `dvage` or with the tenure breakdown in Section 5 to see whether the effect concentrates among older, larger-home households specifically). Among the new covariates, `large_family` (OR 1.68) and `lone_parent` (OR 1.22) both raise vulnerability odds as expected, and `workless_household` (OR 1.23) is directionally consistent with `jbstat_security`'s protective effect.

**Driver effect sizes vary substantially by region** (`driver_analysis_by_region.csv`, `driver_analysis_by_region.png`). Financial strain's odds ratio ranges from 14.68 in Northern Ireland and 12.14 in South East down to just 3.02 in London — a roughly 4.9× spread. This is a striking finding in its own right: financial strain matters *more* as a driver of fuel vulnerability in Northern Ireland and South East than it does in London, even though these three areas otherwise sit at very different points on the overall vulnerability-prevalence ranking (NI highest, South East low, London mid-low). One plausible reading is that London's fuel-vulnerability variation is driven relatively more by structural factors (housing costs, tenure mix) than by household-level financial strain specifically, while in NI and the South East, a given increment of financial strain translates into a larger jump in fuel-vulnerability risk — worth further investigation if a region-specific policy response is being designed. Smaller-sample regions (Northern Ireland, n=1,878; North East, n=4,577) carry correspondingly wider uncertainty around their region-specific coefficients, a caveat worth remembering before treating any single region's driver profile as precisely estimated.

## 5. Geography, Ethnicity, Disability, and Tenure

### 5.1 Regional pattern

Northern Ireland (15.62%) is the standout, more than 5.8 percentage points above the next-highest region (West Midlands, 9.75%), and roughly 2.6× the lowest (South East, 5.96%) — `policy_vulnerability_by_region.csv`, `policy_vulnerability_by_region_map.png`. The Midlands/North/Wales form a contiguous mid-vulnerability band, while the southern English regions (South East, South West, East of England) sit persistently lowest across nearly every regional table in this project — the by-wave breakdown, the temporal-change map (`policy_temporal_change_map.png`, where South West is the *only* region to worsen, +0.23pp, while Northern Ireland improves by far the most, −10.21pp), and both policy-geography maps in Stage 4.

### 5.2 Ethnicity, disability, and tenure

**Ethnicity, disability, and tenure — added this session specifically to make the project's outputs comparable to external poverty statistics — reveal a genuinely informative, non-uniform picture.** By ethnicity (`policy_vulnerability_by_ethnicity.csv`), Black Caribbean households rank highest (14.06%, n=6,751) and Any other Asian background lowest (6.66%, n=2,570); White households, who make up the large majority of the sample (265,950 of ~339k rows), sit toward the lower end (7.76%). By disability (`policy_vulnerability_by_disability.csv`), households containing a disabled adult are more vulnerable (12.86%, n=14,119) than those without (10.33%, n=15,840) — directionally as expected, though note the much smaller sample here (disability is observed only for responding adults, not the full household). By tenure (`policy_vulnerability_by_tenure.csv`), the ranking is Social renting (11.28%) > **Owned outright (9.87%)** > Private renting (8.50%) > Buying with mortgage (4.15%) — with Owned outright's position above Private renting being the one genuinely surprising result: a fuel-to-income measure, unlike an income-poverty measure, picks up asset-rich-but-heating-cost-exposed households (often older, outright owners in older housing stock) that a pure income lens does not flag as poor. "Other" tenure (13.10%, n=1,173 — a small residual category) is highest of all five tenure groups, though its small sample size means this particular number should be treated with more caution than the four main categories.

These three breakdowns were deliberately built as descriptive strata, not folded into the SEM/CVAE as new latent constructs and not added as covariates to the existing driver-analysis regression — ethnicity and disability are demographic/health covariates, not reflective indicators of an underlying continuous resource construct, and several ethnicity categories are small-sample nationally (e.g. Chinese n=1,408, Any other Black background n=426), which would have destabilized an already-validated regression for uncertain benefit.

### 5.2b Family composition and employment status — new this iteration

**Unlike ethnicity/disability/tenure above, family composition and employment status *do* flow into the driver-analysis regression** (as `large_family`/`lone_parent`/`workless_household`, Section 4) — they are theoretically upstream causes of resource stock, not just descriptive strata, matching JRF's own framework (*UK Poverty 2025*, Table 5 p.36 for family type; p.76 for work status).

**By family composition** (`policy_vulnerability_by_family_composition.csv`, 6 categories): Lone parent with 1–2 children is highest (15.13%, n=16,378), followed by Lone parent with 3+ children (13.18%, n=2,165); No children sits in the middle (9.00%, n=229,491, the large majority of the panel); Couple with 3+ children (5.27%), Other multi-adult with children (4.32%), and Couple with 1–2 children (3.67%, the lowest) round out the ranking. The gap is not a smooth function of household size alone — both lone-parent categories sit well above both couple categories regardless of child count, consistent with single-earner households carrying materially higher fuel-to-income risk than two-adult households. This directly supports `lone_parent`'s significant, positive driver-regression coefficient (OR 1.22, Section 4) and independently reproduces the *shape* (not just the presence) of JRF's own lone-parent-vs-couple poverty gap.

**By employment status** (`policy_vulnerability_by_employment.csv`, 3 categories): a clean, monotonic gradient from Workless household (14.54%, n=124,412) through Part-time only (9.29%, n=36,234) down to Full-time or self-employed (3.25%, n=176,250, the lowest and largest group) — roughly a 4.5× spread between the highest and lowest employment-status categories, the largest ratio of any single-dimension breakdown in this section. This is the cleanest, most monotonic gradient of any demographic stratification in the whole project, and directly supports `workless_household`'s driver-regression coefficient (OR 1.23).

Both breakdowns collapse to a 2-category version (`policy_vulnerability_by_family_type_collapsed.csv`, `policy_vulnerability_by_work_status_collapsed.csv`) for the JRF external-validation comparison in Section 6.

### 5.2c Robustness check: does household size distort the vulnerability target? — new this iteration

The project's primary `fuel_to_income_ratio` target is deliberately **unequivalised** (mirroring the UK's own official 10%-of-income fuel-poverty definition), unlike JRF's own equivalised income-poverty measure — raising an obvious question: is the unequivalised choice silently misclassifying households by size? `run_equivalisation_robustness_check` (`reports/06_methodology.md` Section 4.2b) answers this directly by re-computing the same ratio with income equivalised (Modified OECD scale, `ieqmoecd_dv`) and checking how often the binary `high_fuel_vulnerable` classification flips.

**The answer is yes, substantially, and the effect is size-graded.** `equivalisation_robustness_check_flips_by_hhsize.csv` shows a clean monotonic staircase: 1-person households never flip (0.0%, n=61,422 — mechanically expected, since equivalising a single-person household's income changes it by a near-1 divisor); 2-person 10.5% (n=88,030); 3-person 16.6% (n=42,006); 4-person 22.9% (n=40,525); **5+-person households flip 42.5% of the time** (n=23,142) — over 4 in 10 large households are classified differently depending on whether income is equivalised. This is a genuine, quantified limitation of the project's deliberate choice to match the UK's official unequivalised standard rather than an oversight: any policy application that specifically targets large households should be aware that the underlying classification is comparatively unstable for exactly that subgroup. The two unsupervised vulnerability scores (fuzzy c-means, one-class SVM) also correlate more weakly against the equivalised ratio (`equivalisation_robustness_check_correlations.csv`: r=0.208/AUC=0.670 and r=0.151/AUC=0.552 respectively) than against the primary unequivalised ratio (Section 4: r=0.262/AUC=0.745 and r=0.172/AUC=0.621) — expected, since neither method was tuned toward the equivalised construct, but a useful quantification of exactly how much the choice of target matters.

### 5.3 The policy geography maps: hotspots, membership shape, and simulated shock exposure

Stage 4's three maps add spatial and distributional nuance beyond the simple ranked bar charts of Section 5.1, and are worth examining together rather than in isolation.

**Map 1 (Resource-to-Stress Hotspot, `map1_resource_stress_hotspot.csv`)** classifies each region into a 3×3 bivariate tier of baseline resource score × vulnerability prevalence. In the current run, Northern Ireland sits in the "High resource / High vulnerability" tier together with Wales and West Midlands — not alone, and not in a "Low resource" tier: Northern Ireland's own baseline resource score (+0.093) is in fact the *highest* of all 12 regions, ahead of Wales (+0.064) and West Midlands (+0.037). This is a genuinely counter-intuitive pairing — the region with the UK's highest fuel vulnerability also has, nominally, the UK's highest baseline-resource score — and it must be read alongside the sign-instability caveat in Section 3.2: because the second-order SEM model's overall sign is not currently identified (Section 8, caveat 5), `baseline_resource_score`'s direction (and hence which end of the tier scale a region falls at) has flipped between refits of this same pipeline — an earlier run placed Northern Ireland in a "Low resource" tier instead. What *is* stable across refits is that Northern Ireland sits at one extreme of the baseline-resource distribution while also having exceptional vulnerability — i.e. its vulnerability is not trivially explained by an equally extreme position at the resource-poor end of the scale either, consistent with the oil-heating mechanism identified in Section 6 rather than a generic resource deficit. North West is currently the only region in the "Mid resource / High vulnerability" tier; North East ("High resource / Mid vulnerability") and Scotland ("Low resource / Mid vulnerability") illustrate how loosely resource tier and vulnerability tier are currently coupled outside Northern Ireland/Wales/West Midlands specifically.

**Map 2 (Fuzzy Membership, `policy_map2_fuzzy_membership.csv`)** reveals a pattern not visible anywhere else in the project: Northern Ireland has the *lowest* mean fuzzy "vulnerable to loss" membership (0.343) of any region, which at first reads as a contradiction of its highest hard-threshold vulnerability rate — until the "% near boundary" column is examined. Every region in Great Britain has 21.7%–27.8% of its households sitting near the fuzzy decision boundary (a genuinely ambiguous, "could go either way" middle group); Northern Ireland has only 8.37%. Northern Ireland's households are sorted much more decisively into "clearly vulnerable" or "clearly not" than anywhere else in the UK — a polarized rather than a graduated distribution. This is a distinct empirical signature from the mean-level finding in Map 1, and together the two maps paint a coherent picture: Northern Ireland doesn't have a moderately elevated risk spread evenly across a wide middle band of households the way other regions do; it has a comparatively sharp binary split, plausibly tracking the oil-heating/non-oil-heating divide identified directly in Section 6 (recall the within-NI comparison: oil users at 20.9% vulnerable, non-oil users at 12.3% — two quite different sub-populations within the same region, rather than one smoothly-varying population).

**Map 3 (Vulnerability Vector Shift, `map3_vulnerability_vector_shift.csv`)** simulates, for every region, the shift in predicted vulnerability probability if each household's own realised FES exposure were replaced by the shared forecast shock. In the current run, region-level shifts are **mixed in sign** — five regions (London, East of England, Scotland, South East, South West) shift slightly positive (rising risk), the other seven shift slightly negative — and uniformly tiny (under 0.015 percentage points everywhere; South West is smallest, London largest). This is a third distinct pattern across successive runs of this same simulation: an earlier draft of this report found uniformly *positive* shifts everywhere, an intermediate run found nearly all regions negative with Northern Ireland largest, and the current run instead finds this mixed, London-largest pattern. The direction — and even which specific region ranks largest or smallest — appears highly sensitive to exactly which realised/forecast FES values get attached to the panel (Stage 2a) and to CVAE training stochasticity (Section 3.4), so this map is best read as evidence that **the shift is consistently small everywhere** (the CVAE-alignment finding in Section 3.4 that the counterfactual's effect is heterogeneous and modest at the household level still holds), rather than as evidence for any specific region's direction or ranking, none of which has proven stable across reruns. Northern Ireland's own shift in the current run is unremarkable in magnitude (mid-ranked, 5th of 12) — a genuine change from earlier drafts of this report, and evidence that this particular statistic simply does not track Northern Ireland's exceptional status on every other measure, not that its status has diminished.

## 6. External Validation Against JRF's UK Poverty 2025

This project's fuel-to-income vulnerability measure and JRF's relative income-poverty rate (after housing costs) are, by design, different constructs — they should broadly agree in direction while disagreeing in specifics, and both kinds of result are informative. The validation (`src/ukhls_external_validation.py`) now checks **six** dimensions plus one time-series comparison — two more than in earlier drafts of this report, made possible only by this iteration's new household-composition and employment features (Section 5.2).

**Family type and work status — new this iteration, and both show full rank agreement.** Collapsing this project's 5-category family-composition breakdown into JRF's own lone-parent-vs-couple-with-children split (Table 5, p.36) and its 3-category employment breakdown into JRF's in-work-vs-not-in-work split (p.76) both give Spearman ρ=1.00 (n=2 each — lone parents and workless households rank higher on both this project's fuel measure and JRF's income measure). These are the first-ever runs of these two comparisons; the ranks agreeing on both is a positive signal, though n=2 is necessarily too small a test to weigh as heavily as region or tenure below.

**Tenure shows the strongest agreement among the higher-n dimensions** (Spearman ρ=0.80, Pearson r=0.68, n=4 — `external_validation_tenure_comparison.csv`, `external_validation_tenure_scatter.png`), with Owned outright as the one notable rank-swap (2nd on this project's measure vs. 3rd on JRF's), consistent with the fuel-vs-income distinction discussed in Section 5.2.

**Region shows strong agreement once Northern Ireland is set aside** (ρ=0.33/r=−0.10 for all 12 regions vs. ρ=0.73/r=0.68 excluding NI — `external_validation_region_comparison.csv`, `external_validation_region_scatter.png`). This is the project's headline validation finding, and it was investigated rather than assumed: Northern Ireland ranks #1 (most vulnerable) on this project's measure but #12 (least income-poor) of 12 UK nations/regions on JRF's own published rate. Rather than treat this as noise, the project verified the mechanism directly against its own data — 71.2% of NI households use oil heating, a rate 7.3× higher than the next-highest GB region (Wales, 9.8%), and, in a controlled within-NI comparison isolating this one factor, oil-heating NI households pay 62% more annually for fuel (£2,017 vs. £1,243) and are vulnerable at nearly double the rate (20.9% vs. 12.3%) of non-oil NI households in the same wave and region (`ni_oil_heating_evidence_by_region.csv`, `ni_oil_heating_evidence_within_ni.csv`). Heating oil is bought in volatile-priced lump sums and sits outside Ofgem's electricity/gas price cap — a genuine, well-isolated fuel-specific cost exposure with no reason to appear in an income-based poverty measure. Northern Ireland is therefore excluded only from the region correlation coefficient (a like-for-like check of construct agreement), never from the underlying regional analysis itself, where its result is arguably the single most policy-relevant finding in the whole regional breakdown, and (as shown in Section 5.3) is corroborated by two further independent Stage 4 findings — its unique hotspot-tier placement and its unusually polarized fuzzy-membership distribution.

**Ethnicity shows the weakest agreement** (Spearman ρ=0.14, Pearson r=0.27, n=6 — `external_validation_ethnicity_comparison.csv`, `external_validation_ethnicity_scatter.png`), and the divergence runs in both directions: Bangladeshi households are JRF's #1 most income-poor ethnic group (56%) but rank only #4 of 6 on this project's fuel measure (9.50%), while Black Caribbean households are the mirror case — #1 on this project's measure (14.06%) but #5 of 6 on JRF's income measure (30%). This is reported as a genuine, unresolved divergence between two different constructs of hardship, worth further investigation (e.g. housing-stock age/type, household composition, or heating-fuel-mix differences by ethnicity — the same class of explanation that resolved the Northern Ireland puzzle above), not dismissed or smoothed over. It is also worth noting explicitly that this project's ethnicity variable is attributed via the household reference person only, and the driver-analysis regression in Section 4 does not include ethnicity as a covariate (see the design rationale in Section 8) — so this divergence, unlike the Northern Ireland one, has not yet been decomposed into a specific mechanism, and represents the clearest open question this body of work raises rather than answers.

**Disability shows directionally consistent but statistically inconclusive agreement** (both measures rank "contains a disabled adult"/"disabled adults" above the alternative, but with only 2 categories the correlation coefficient is mathematically undefined — `external_validation_disability_comparison.csv` — and both figures display "ρ=nan"/"r=nan" in their titles, a technically correct but potentially confusing result that would benefit from an explanatory caption if shown to a non-technical audience).

**The wave-level time trend independently reproduces JRF's own account of the cost-of-living crisis** (`external_validation_wave_trend.png`). JRF's own slow-moving annual relative-poverty measure stayed "broadly flat" 2021/22→2022/23 by its own account, while its faster cost-of-living hardship tracker showed the share of low-income households going without essentials peaking around October 2022 before easing only partially by October 2024. This project's fuel-to-income vulnerability rate — an independently constructed measure — shows the same shape: 6.31% (2021) → 10.83% (2022) → 10.62% (2023), a sharp spike followed by only partial easing, matching JRF's *faster*, more responsive indicator rather than its slower one. This is a meaningful validation that the measure is sensitive to the right real-world event at the right time, and it is the one external-validation result in this project that does not require any caveat or exception to hold — it agrees cleanly.

## 7. Forward-Looking Prediction

Stage 5 answers a genuinely different, prospective question from every earlier stage: not who is vulnerable now, but who is about to become vulnerable, using only information available before the outcome is known.

**The model discriminates well on data it never trained on.** Walk-forward validated on the two most recent held-out wave transitions (m→n, n→o), the logistic model achieves **AUC 0.7615** against the true next-wave vulnerability outcome (`stage5_validation_metrics.csv`, `stage5_validation_roc.png`) — a solid result for a cross-sectional predictor of a genuinely future, unobserved-at-training-time outcome. Its correlation with the continuous fuel-to-income ratio is weaker (Pearson r=0.272), suggesting the model is considerably better at classifying who crosses the vulnerability threshold than at explaining the magnitude of financial strain. Underlying this validation, 261,759 raw household-wave transition pairs across all 14 waves reduce to 177,408 usable training rows and 21,161 usable validation rows after removing incomplete cases (`stage5_transition_pairs.csv`) — a roughly 24% attrition rate from missingness that is worth remembering as a scope caveat on the validated model.

**The forecast signal (`fes_magnitude`) is a real, significant, but comparatively modest predictor in the final model** (odds ratio 1.07, p<0.0001, `stage5_driver_coefficients.csv`) — this project's earlier drafts reported a far larger coefficient (OR ≈52) for this same predictor, which reflected the same fes_delta/fes_selected data-pipeline bug corrected in Section 8; the current, corrected value is consistent in direction and significance but a much smaller, more plausible effect size (and has moved slightly, from an intermediate OR≈1.03 to the current 1.07, consistent with normal run-to-run refit variation on a model that is genuinely re-estimated, not with any further defect). `financial_strain_score` (OR 4.98) remains by far the dominant driver, as in the contemporaneous Stage 3 analysis. Of the three new household-composition/employment covariates added this iteration, `lone_parent` (OR 1.53) and `workless_household` (OR 1.43) are both significant, positive risk factors, consistent with their equivalent roles in the Stage 3 driver model (Section 4) and the standalone family-composition/employment breakdowns (Section 5.2b); `large_family` (OR 0.96) is **not statistically significant** (p=0.456) in this prospective model, unlike Stage 3's contemporaneous driver model, where it is significant (OR 1.68) — a genuine divergence between the contemporaneous and prospective specifications, plausibly because family size at wave *t* is a weaker predictor of *next-year* vulnerability than of *current* vulnerability, though this has not been tested directly. Three of the four COR-SEM factor scores (`energy_score` OR 0.31, `condition_score` OR 0.89, `personal_score` OR 0.93) behave exactly as "more baseline resource → lower future risk" predicts; `object_score` (OR 1.42) is the one consistent exception, raising rather than lowering predicted future risk — the same pattern flagged in Section 4's contemporaneous driver analysis, now confirmed prospectively too.

**Applying the model to the most recent wave produces a right-skewed risk distribution with a small, concentrated high-risk group.** Mean predicted probability is 7.06%, but the median is only 4.02% — most households are low-risk, while 218 households (1.14%) are predicted above 50% probability and 3,748 (19.6%) above 10% (`stage5_forward_predictions.csv`) — figures essentially unchanged in shape from an earlier run (220/1.15% and 3,710/19.4% respectively — normal refit-to-refit variation, not a substantive shift), with the underlying `fes_magnitude_used` signal genuinely varying by household (24 distinct values) rather than being a single constant, a data-quality improvement discussed in Section 8. This concentrated tail is an obvious candidate for targeted intervention, distinct from a purely geographic targeting approach.

**Regionally and seasonally, the forward predictions largely reproduce known patterns rather than reshuffling them.** Northern Ireland is again highest (7.98%) and South West lowest (6.36%) in the forward-predicted map (`stage5_forward_prediction_map.csv`/`.png`) — the same ordering as the historical prevalence data, just compressed in range (1.61 percentage points forward vs. 9.66 points historically, reflecting model shrinkage toward the forecast-shock term's own, now genuinely household-varying but still comparatively narrow, range). By target month (`stage5_forward_prediction_by_month.csv`/`.png`), October (7.57%), April (7.41%), and November (7.40%) are the highest-risk target months, and September (6.70%), August (6.78%), and June (6.81%) the lowest — a 0.87-percentage-point spread, essentially unchanged from an earlier run's 0.86-point spread and the same April/October/November-high, low-single-digit-months-low pattern, even though the model has been refit and several individual coefficients (above) shifted modestly.

## 8. Cross-Cutting Methodological Caveats

These are collected here for visibility; each is detailed with exact numbers in the relevant section above and in the file-by-file catalog (`reports/01_outputs_catalog.md`).

**Changelog — two data-pipeline fixes made this iteration, and why several numbers above differ from earlier drafts:**

- **A crash in `attach_fes_delta`** (`KeyError: 'fes_selected'`) was fixed — a column-rename step had attached `fes_selected` but not its sibling `fes_weighted`, and the earlier, un-fixed code path affected how `fes_delta`/`fes_magnitude` were resolved for a full pipeline run. This is the most likely reason `fes_delta`'s significance flipped between drafts (Sections 3.3, 4, 7) and why Stage 5's `fes_magnitude` coefficient shrank from an implausible OR≈52 to a much more plausible OR≈1.07 (Section 7) — both changes are corrections toward more trustworthy numbers, not regressions.
- **One household-wave row with zero observed values across all 13 COR-SEM indicators** was degenerating into a 0×0 covariance submatrix inside `semopy`'s FIML fit, producing hundreds of spurious LAPACK error lines per run without affecting any actual estimate (confirmed by direct before/after comparison) — now dropped before fitting. Purely a log-noise fix, mentioned here for completeness.

**Remaining caveats:**

1. Stage 1's model-selection hindsight problem is fixed (default `selection_basis='validation'`, never hindsight), but the underlying backtest-vs-realised-accuracy disagreement it exposed is still a genuine property of the forecasting problem, not fully resolved by honest selection alone (Section 2.1).
2. The 16-year rolling comparison (`Equal_Macro` wins) and the single most-recent-year comparison (`Equal_Selected`/`Equal_Weighted` win) disagree on which composite FES variant is "best" — reported as an open question rather than reconciled (Section 2.2).
3. `FES_core`/`FES_macro` get 2025's basic stress *direction* wrong (positive/near-zero all year against a genuinely negative realised actual); only `FES_selected`/`FES_weighted` land on the correct side, though both still understate its magnitude (Section 2.3).
4. Two SEM fit-index tables contain values outside their mathematically valid range (CFI/TLI outside [0,1] in one; an implausible SRMR in the other), and CFI/TLI themselves vary run-to-run (observed range: CFI −0.14 to −0.54).
5. **The second-order SEM structural model's sign is not identified** — three of four first-order factors load at |standardized loading| ≈1.0 on the second-order Baseline factor with a sign that flips between refits, which means the Baseline score downstream should be read as directionally unresolved, not merely numerically imbalanced (Section 3.2, upgraded from an earlier, milder framing of this same issue).
6. **The FES-moderation interaction term is now statistically significant in every recent refit (p<0.0001), but its sign is not stable across those refits** (+0.00049 then −0.00073) — a materially different, and more specific, caveat than an earlier draft's "not significant, p=0.608" finding. Only the two main effects (`fes_delta`, `baseline_score`) are currently trustworthy; the interaction itself is not yet either confirmed or refuted (Section 3.3).
7. The CVAE's latent space now aligns cleanly with **three** of the four intended SEM factors (an improvement for object/condition/personal); the fourth (z4/energy) has moved from partially entangled to actively mis-aligned with `object_score`, a worse instance of the same issue flagged in an earlier draft (Section 3.4).
8. `object_score` is a genuine, reproducible exception to "more baseline resource → lower vulnerability" — it raises rather than lowers both contemporaneous (Section 4) and forward-predicted (Section 7) vulnerability risk, plausibly reflecting the heating cost of larger/older owned housing.
9. Ethnicity's divergence from JRF's income-poverty measure (Section 6) has not been decomposed into a specific mechanism the way Northern Ireland's has, and remains the project's clearest open question.
10. Two output tables are byte-identical duplicates (`policy_map_baseline_resource_by_region.csv` and `policy_map_financial_strain_by_region.csv`).
11. A likely data-sparsity artifact appears in the region×year heatmap (Northern Ireland's 2024 cell reads exactly 0.0%).
12. Ethnicity is attributed via the household reference person only (not every household member), and disability is observed only for responding adults — both are documented simplifications, not full-household measures.
13. Stage 5's usable model sample reflects roughly 24% attrition from missingness across the 14 wave-to-wave transitions, a scope caveat on the validated forward-prediction results.
14. Family type and work status (Section 6) are validated against JRF for the first time this iteration, each with only n=2 categories — a necessarily weak test of rank agreement, reported as a positive but preliminary signal, not on the same footing as region or tenure's larger-n comparisons.
15. `large_family` is a significant risk factor in Stage 3's contemporaneous driver model (OR 1.68) but **not significant** in Stage 5's prospective forward-prediction model (OR 0.96, p=0.456) — a genuine contemporaneous-vs-prospective divergence for this one covariate, not yet explained (Section 7).
16. The household-vulnerability target is deliberately unequivalised for household size; a robustness check confirms this materially affects classification specifically for large households (0.0% flip rate at 1-person households rising to 42.5% at 5+-person households under an equivalised alternative) — a quantified, transparent limitation of a deliberate design choice, not an oversight (Section 5.2c).

None of these caveats overturn the project's central, cross-validated findings (the cost-of-living-crisis spike, the strength of financial strain as a driver, FES's own robust significance across every stage that tests its main effect, Northern Ireland's fuel-oil-driven outlier status — corroborated across four independent stages — or the Stage 5 model's genuine forward discriminative power) — but they should inform exactly how confidently specific secondary numbers (particular SEM fit statistics, the FES-*moderation* interaction specifically as distinct from FES's main effect, the FES_macro-vs-core tension) are quoted in any external-facing use of this work.

## 9. What This Body of Work Establishes

Taken together, the five stages support a coherent account: household fuel vulnerability in the UK is driven predominantly by household-level financial circumstances, with the forecasted macro energy-price shock (FES) contributing a real, consistently significant, but comparatively modest independent effect on top — not the "not significant at all" reading an earlier draft of this work reported. Fuel vulnerability has a clear geographic and demographic structure that partly but not fully overlaps with income-poverty as conventionally measured, with Northern Ireland's oil-heating exposure standing out — corroborated across region-level, fuzzy-membership, vector-shift, and external-validation analyses alike — as the clearest case where a fuel-specific lens sees something an income-based lens does not; and a model trained only on information available before the fact can meaningfully anticipate, a year ahead, which households are moving toward vulnerability — with real, if compressed, geographic and seasonal structure, and a small concentrated group carrying disproportionate predicted risk. Of Hobfoll's four COR resource dimensions, three (Condition, Personal, Energy) behave exactly as theorized wherever tested; the fourth (Object) consistently runs the opposite way, a genuine and reproducible finding rather than noise; and whether resources specifically *moderate* an FES shock — as opposed to each mattering independently — remains genuinely unresolved, sign-unstable across refits, and should not yet be reported as either confirmed or refuted. The external validation against JRF's independently-produced statistics, now spanning six dimensions rather than four, is not perfect, but where it disagrees, the disagreement is itself informative rather than merely noisy (most clearly for Northern Ireland, where the mechanism is fully traced; less clearly for ethnicity, where it remains an open question), and where it agrees, it agrees on level (tenure, region-excluding-NI, and now family type and work status), on timing (the wave-level cost-of-living-crisis trend), and increasingly broadly across dimensions — a meaningfully stronger form of validation than internal consistency checks alone could provide.
